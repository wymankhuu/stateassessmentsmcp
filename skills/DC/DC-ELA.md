## 1. Overview

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Assessment Name** | DC Comprehensive Assessment Program in ELA (DC CAPE) — English Language Arts/Literacy |
| **Administering Agency** | Office of the State Superintendent of Education (OSSE) |
| **Vendor** | Pearson |
| **Grades Tested** | 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11 |
| **Testing Window** | March – May |
| **Delivery** | Computer-based; multi-session test |
| **Standards** | DC Common Core State Standards (DC-CCSS) — ELA |
| **Score Scale** | 650–850 scale score (varies by grade); 5 performance levels |
| **Reporting** | Individual Student Report; School, LEA, District aggregates |

> **DC CAPE and PARCC:** DC CAPE ELA is derived from the **Partnership for Assessment of Readiness for College and Careers (PARCC)** design. Item types, task models, and rubrics reflect the PARCC architecture: Literary Analysis Task (LAT), Research Simulation Task (RST), and Narrative Writing Task (NWT). DC uses **DC Common Core State Standards (DC-CCSS)**, which are aligned to CCSS ELA, with passages reflecting the District of Columbia's history, government, culture, and landmarks.

### Performance Levels

| Level | Label | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | **Exceeded Expectations** | Student demonstrates distinguished command of the knowledge and skills; advanced analysis and evidence use |
| 4 | **Met Expectations** | Student demonstrates strong command of the knowledge and skills; thorough analysis with relevant evidence |
| 3 | **Approached Expectations** | Student demonstrates moderate command; partial analysis with some evidence use |
| 2 | **Partially Met Expectations** | Student demonstrates partial command; limited analysis and inconsistent evidence |
| 1 | **Did Not Yet Meet Expectations** | Student demonstrates minimal command; significant gaps in grade-level expectations |

## 2. Standards Framework

> **Reference:** See `_shared/ccss_ela_framework.md` for full CCR Anchor Standards and grade-by-grade progressions. DC uses the **DC Common Core State Standards (DC-CCSS)** for ELA. Standards are referenced using DC-CCSS codes (e.g., DC-CCSS.ELA.RL.3.1).

### Reading Literature (RL) — Key Standards by Grade Band

| Standard | Gr 3–5 Focus | Gr 6–8 Focus | Gr 11 Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DC-CCSS.RL.1** — Key details & inference | Explicit details; simple inference from text | Cite textual evidence; analyze what text says explicitly vs. inferentially | Cite strong and thorough textual evidence to support analysis |
| **DC-CCSS.RL.2** — Theme / central idea | Determine message or lesson; summarize | Analyze theme development; how it emerges through characters and plot | Analyze how themes develop and interact over the course of a text |
| **DC-CCSS.RL.3** — Character, setting, events | Describe characters' traits and responses | Analyze how dialogue and incidents reveal character and advance plot | Analyze impact of author's choices regarding story elements |
| **DC-CCSS.RL.4** — Word meaning | Determine meaning of words; recognize figurative language | Analyze figurative and connotative meanings; word choice and tone | Determine multiple meanings; analyze how word choice shapes meaning and tone |
| **DC-CCSS.RL.6** — Point of view | Identify narrator's point of view | Analyze how differences in POV create effects like suspense or humor | Analyze a case in which grasping point of view requires distinguishing the author's from the characters' |

### Reading Informational Text (RI) — Key Standards by Grade Band

| Standard | Gr 3–5 Focus | Gr 6–8 Focus | Gr 11 Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DC-CCSS.RI.1** — Key details & inference | Quote accurately; draw inferences | Cite textual evidence supporting analysis | Cite strong and thorough textual evidence; determine where text leaves matters uncertain |
| **DC-CCSS.RI.3** — Connections & relationships | Explain relationships between events, ideas, or concepts | Analyze how a text makes connections between ideas | Analyze a complex set of ideas or sequence of events |
| **DC-CCSS.RI.6** — Author's purpose & point of view | Identify author's purpose | Determine author's point of view; analyze how purpose shapes content | Determine an author's point of view or purpose; analyze rhetoric |
| **DC-CCSS.RI.8** — Argument & evidence | Explain how reasons support points | Trace and evaluate argument; assess whether reasoning is sound | Delineate and evaluate reasoning in seminal U.S. texts |
| **DC-CCSS.RI.9** — Compare/integrate information | Compare multiple texts on same topic | Analyze how two texts treat similar topics differently | Analyze foundational U.S. documents of historical and literary significance |

### Writing Standards

| Standard | Focus | DOK |
|---|---|---|
| **DC-CCSS.W.1** — Argument/Opinion | Write arguments with clear claims, evidence, and reasoning | 3–4 |
| **DC-CCSS.W.2** — Informative/Explanatory | Develop topics with facts, details, definitions, examples | 3 |
| **DC-CCSS.W.3** — Narrative | Establish situation; develop narrator/characters; use narrative techniques | 3–4 |
| **DC-CCSS.W.7** — Research | Conduct short research projects; gather relevant information | 3 |

## 3. Test Structure

DC CAPE ELA uses the PARCC task model architecture with three distinct task types administered across multiple sessions.

```
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
|              DC CAPE ELA STRUCTURE (Grades 3–8, 11)               |
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  LITERARY ANALYSIS TASK (LAT)                                    |
|  ~75–90 minutes                                                   |
|                                                                  |
|  1–2 literary passages                                            |
|  Items: SR, MS, EBSR, TE                                         |
|  Prose Constructed Response (PCR) — literary analysis essay       |
|                                                                  |
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  RESEARCH SIMULATION TASK (RST)                                  |
|  ~75–90 minutes                                                   |
|                                                                  |
|  2–3 informational source texts (articles, data, excerpts)       |
|  Items: SR, MS, EBSR, TE                                         |
|  Prose Constructed Response (PCR) — research-based essay          |
|                                                                  |
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  NARRATIVE WRITING TASK (NWT)                                    |
|  ~75–90 minutes                                                   |
|                                                                  |
|  1 literary or informational stimulus passage                     |
|  Items: SR, MS, EBSR, TE                                         |
|  Prose Constructed Response (PCR) — narrative essay               |
|                                                                  |
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

## 5. Item Types & Formats

### Selected Response (SR)

```
+----------------------------------------------------------+
|  SELECTED RESPONSE — 1 point                             |
|                                                          |
|  4 options (A, B, C, D)                                  |
|  Passage-dependent; always tied to a stimulus            |
|  Machine scored                                          |
|  DOK 2–3                                                 |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
```

**SR Example (Grade 6 Informational, DOK 2, DC-CCSS.RI.6.6):**

*[After reading an article about the history and purpose of the National Mall]*

What is the author's MAIN purpose in writing this article?

A. To persuade readers that the National Mall should be expanded to include more memorials
B. To entertain readers with dramatic stories about the construction of the Washington Monument
C. To inform readers about how the National Mall has served as America's gathering place for civic expression and national identity for over two centuries
D. To warn readers about the challenges of maintaining aging monuments and memorials

*Correct Answer: C*
*Alignment: DC-CCSS.ELA.RI.6.6 | Reading Informational Text | DOK 2*

---

### Multi-Select Response (MS)

```
+----------------------------------------------------------+
|  MULTI-SELECT RESPONSE — 1 point                         |
|                                                          |
|  5–8 options; select exactly 2 or 3 correct answers      |
|  Full credit only; partial credit not awarded            |
|  Machine scored                                          |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
```

**MS Example (Grade 5 Literary, DOK 2, DC-CCSS.RL.5.3):**

*[After reading a short story about a student visiting the Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture for the first time]*

Select the TWO details from the story that BEST show how the narrator's understanding of history changes during the museum visit.

A. "Our teacher told us to stay with our groups and meet at the cafeteria for lunch."
B. "Before the trip, I thought history was just dates and names in a textbook, nothing that had anything to do with me."
C. "Standing in front of Emmett Till's casket, reading the words his mother had spoken, I realized that history wasn't something that happened to other people — it was something that shaped my own life."
D. "The museum building was designed with a bronze-colored corona inspired by Yoruba art."
E. "We took the bus from our school in Southeast DC to the National Mall."

*Correct Answers: B, C*
*Alignment: DC-CCSS.ELA.RL.5.3 | Reading Literary Text | DOK 2*

---

### Evidence-Based Selected Response (EBSR)

```
+----------------------------------------------------------+
|  EVIDENCE-BASED SELECTED RESPONSE — 2 points            |
|                                                          |
|  Part A: Inference or analysis (1 pt)                   |
|  Part B: Text evidence supporting Part A (1 pt)          |
|  Machine scored                                          |
|  DOK 2–3                                                 |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
```

**EBSR Example (Grade 8 Informational, DOK 3, DC-CCSS.RI.8.8):**

*[After reading an argumentative essay about whether DC should be granted full statehood]*

**Part A:** Which statement BEST describes the author's central argument in this essay?

A. DC already has sufficient representation through its delegate in the House of Representatives
B. DC residents deserve full statehood because they pay federal taxes, serve in the military, and are subject to federal law, yet lack voting representation in Congress
C. DC should remain a federal district because the Constitution specifically designates it as the seat of government
D. The statehood issue is primarily about political advantage rather than fair representation

**Part B:** Which sentence from the essay BEST supports your answer to Part A?

A. "The District of Columbia was established by the Residence Act of 1790."
B. "Several proposals have suggested retroceding most of DC back to Maryland."
C. "With over 700,000 residents paying more per capita in federal taxes than any state, yet having no voting voice in the body that determines how those taxes are spent, DC's situation represents the very taxation without representation that sparked the nation's founding."
D. "The Twenty-Third Amendment, ratified in 1961, granted DC residents the right to vote in presidential elections."

*Correct Answers: Part A: B | Part B: C*
*Alignment: DC-CCSS.ELA.RI.8.8 | Reading Informational Text | DOK 3*

### Prose Constructed Response (PCR)

```
+----------------------------------------------------------+
|  PROSE CONSTRUCTED RESPONSE — 0–4 points                 |
|                                                          |
|  Multi-paragraph written response to passage(s)         |
|  LAT: Literary analysis essay                            |
|  RST: Research-based argumentative/informational essay   |
|  NWT: Narrative continuation or original narrative       |
|  Scored on a single 0–4 holistic rubric                  |
|  Human scored by trained raters                          |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
```

**PCR Prompt Example — RST (Grade 7, DC-CCSS.W.7.1):**

*[Sources provided: Article 1 on the Smithsonian Institution's founding and mission; Article 2 on the debate over which stories and artifacts should be displayed in national museums; Data table on annual Smithsonian museum attendance by museum]*

You have read three sources about the Smithsonian Institution and the challenge of deciding what to include in the nation's museums. Write an essay that argues whether the Smithsonian should prioritize digital access to its collections over building new physical exhibits. Use evidence from at least two of the three sources to support your argument. Be sure to acknowledge a counterclaim.

**PCR Prompt Example — NWT (Grade 5, DC-CCSS.W.5.3):**

*[After reading a story about Frederick Douglass arriving in Washington, DC, after the Civil War]*

The story ends with Frederick Douglass walking along the streets of Washington, DC, for the first time as a free man in the nation's capital. Write a continuation of the story that describes what happens next. Use details from the passage to develop the characters and setting, and include descriptive details that help the reader picture the scene.

## 8. Scoring Rubrics

### Prose Constructed Response (PCR) Rubric — 0–4 Points (Holistic)

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| 4 | **Thorough understanding** of the text(s). Response is well-organized and includes specific, relevant evidence. Analysis is accurate, insightful, and fully developed. For narrative: effective use of narrative techniques, sensory details, and clear event sequence. Conventions errors minimal and do not interfere. |
| 3 | **Adequate understanding** of the text(s). Response is organized and includes relevant evidence, though analysis may not be fully developed. For narrative: adequate narrative techniques with generally clear sequence. Some conventions errors but meaning is clear. |
| 2 | **Partial understanding** of the text(s). Response includes some evidence but analysis is superficial or uneven. Organization may be inconsistent. For narrative: limited narrative techniques; sequence may be unclear. Conventions errors may occasionally interfere with meaning. |
| 1 | **Minimal understanding** of the text(s). Response includes little or no relevant evidence. Analysis is vague or inaccurate. For narrative: minimal narrative development. Frequent conventions errors interfere with meaning. |
| 0 | Blank, copied text, off-topic, or entirely in a language other than English. |

### PCR Scoring Notes

- **Evidence from text is required.** Responses relying solely on prior knowledge without referencing provided passages earn a maximum of 1 point.
- **RST PCR** must integrate evidence from at least two sources for a score of 3 or 4.
- **NWT PCR** must use details from the stimulus passage and demonstrate narrative techniques (dialogue, description, pacing).

## 11. Quality Checklist

### Pre-Generation Checklist

- [ ] DC-CCSS standard code verified
- [ ] PARCC task type correctly identified (LAT, RST, or NWT)
- [ ] EBSR Part B is a quote that directly supports Part A (not just same topic)
- [ ] MS items specify exact number of answers to select (TWO/THREE)
- [ ] PCR prompt is open-ended enough for student voice
- [ ] PCR scored on 0–4 holistic rubric (not trait-based)
- [ ] Passage Lexile matches grade-band range
- [ ] RST sources include at least one non-prose source (graph, data table, image)
- [ ] DC context incorporated where appropriate (National Mall, Smithsonian, statehood, civil rights)
- [ ] Distractors represent authentic student misconceptions (not trivially wrong)
- [ ] DOK level matches the actual cognitive demand of the question
- [ ] Grade 11 items reflect appropriate text complexity and analytical depth

### Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| EBSR Part B that supports a different answer | Part B must directly support Part A's correct answer | Cross-check: does Part B quote only make sense given Part A answer? |
| PCR prompts with only one correct interpretation | Essay needs room for student perspective | Keep prompts debatable or open-ended |
| Using 12-point trait-based FW rubric instead of 4-point holistic PCR | DC CAPE uses PARCC-style 0–4 holistic PCR rubric | Always use 0–4 holistic scale |
| RST sources all the same format (three articles) | PARCC RST includes varied source types | Include data tables, graphs, or images as at least one RST source |
| Forgetting Grade 11 | DC CAPE tests Grade 11 unlike some states | Include Grade 11 in test specifications |
| Generic mid-Atlantic context | DC has distinct political, cultural, and historical identity | Use National Mall, Smithsonian, DC statehood, Frederick Douglass contexts |

## 12. Prompt Patterns

### Pattern 1: Central Idea / Theme (SR or EBSR, DOK 2–3)
> "What [central idea / theme] does the [passage / author] develop? Which detail from the text BEST supports this [idea / theme]?"

### Pattern 2: Author's Craft (SR, DOK 3)
> "How does the author's use of [figurative language / structure / word choice / point of view] in [this section / paragraph #] affect the [meaning / tone / reader's understanding of ____]?"

### Pattern 3: Evidence Selection (EBSR Part B)
> "Which [sentence / detail] from the [passage] BEST supports your answer to Part A?"

### Pattern 4: Research Simulation — Multi-Source Argument (PCR, DOK 4)
> "Using evidence from at least two of the three sources, write an argumentative essay about [DC-relevant debatable issue]. Be sure to acknowledge a counterclaim."

### Pattern 5: Narrative Writing Task (PCR, DOK 3–4)
> "Write a [continuation / narrative] based on the passage. Use details from the text to develop [characters / setting / plot]. Include narrative techniques such as dialogue, description, and pacing."

### Pattern 6: Literary Analysis Task (PCR, DOK 3–4)
> "Write an essay analyzing how the author uses [literary device / structure / characterization] to develop [theme / central idea] in the passage. Use specific evidence from the text to support your analysis."

### Pattern 7: Multi-Select Evidence (MS, DOK 2)
> "Select the TWO [details / sentences / examples] from the text that BEST [support / show / explain] [specific focus]."

### Pattern 8: Compare Texts (SR or EBSR, DOK 3)
> "Both sources discuss [common topic]. How does [Source 1] treat this topic differently from [Source 2]?"
